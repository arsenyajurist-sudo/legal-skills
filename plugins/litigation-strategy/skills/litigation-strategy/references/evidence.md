# Evidence in civil and commercial disputes

Rules of evidence differ by system and forum; test everything below against the real rules and mark specifics `[VERIFY]`.

## For every key piece of evidence ask
- **Admissible?** Obtained lawfully, from a proper source, within the rules on hearsay, privilege, and authentication?
- **Relevant?** Does it prove the disputed fact or only something adjacent?
- **Reliable?** Can authenticity, authorship, date, method be attacked?
- **Sufficient?** Enough alone, or only as part of a body of evidence?
- **Weight:** what will the court actually do with it?

## Documents
- Originals vs copies; best-evidence or equivalent rules; certified or notarized copies.
- Signatures: forgery allegations require a plan (handwriting expert, specimen signatures) and have costs consequences if they fail.
- Required form of a transaction (writing, notarization, registration): consequences of non-compliance, including restrictions on proving the contract by witnesses.
- Business records: foundation and custodian; contemporaneous records beat later reconstructions.

## Electronic evidence, messaging, websites
- Preserve early and properly: forensic copy, metadata, screenshots with URL and timestamp, notarized or certified capture, or a trusted third-party capture service.
- Authenticate: who wrote it, from which account, with what proof of control of the account or device.
- Chain of custody and risk of editing; export formats that keep metadata.
- Covert recordings: legality varies widely by jurisdiction; check before relying on one and before making one.

## Witnesses
- Personal knowledge only; interest and bias; consistency with documents; memory over time.
- Witness statements and cross-examination: prepare for the three questions that will sink the witness.
- Hearsay and its exceptions; character and similar-fact evidence; opinion evidence from lay witnesses.
- Compellability, depositions or evidence on commission for witnesses abroad.

## Experts
- Qualification and independence; the duty to the court; the instructions given; the method and its acceptance (admissibility standards differ).
- Scope: do not let the expert decide legal questions; do not accept questions that let the other side's expert define the issue.
- Counter-expert, joint statements, and challenge on method rather than on credentials.
- Timing: expert deadlines are strict, and late reports are excluded or costed.

## Disclosure, discovery, and compelled evidence
- Know the regime: standard disclosure, depositions, interrogatories, requests for admission, subpoenas to third parties, orders against non-parties.
- Target specific categories; justify each; propose proportionate search terms and date ranges.
- Privilege review before production: legal advice and litigation privilege or work product; inadvertent waiver rules.
- Sanctions for default or destruction (adverse inference, striking out, costs): and your own duty to preserve.

## Admissions, estoppel, and preclusion
- Admissions in pleadings, correspondence, and prior proceedings; formal requests to admit.
- Facts already decided between the same parties (issue estoppel, res judicata, collateral estoppel) and facts established by criminal convictions in some systems `[VERIFY]`.
- Judicial notice and presumptions.

## Fabrication or forgery
- If the other side's document looks fabricated: preserve the original, request disclosure of the native file, instruct an expert, and consider whether to plead fraud (high standard, costs and professional-conduct consequences).
- If your own client's document is attacked: assess honestly before the hearing; never present a document you have reason to think is false.

## What to do with a hole in the evidence
List it, then choose: obtain (disclosure, subpoena, request, expert), replace (other evidence of the same fact), reframe (a line of position that does not need it), concede (and bargain for something), or settle before the hole is exposed. Never paper over a hole with a witness statement that contradicts the documents.
