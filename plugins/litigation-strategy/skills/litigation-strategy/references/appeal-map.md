# Appeal map

Appeal systems differ widely: tiers, permission requirements, deadlines, and standards. Establish the forum's route first, then use the logic below. Mark every specific rule `[VERIFY]`.

## 1. Establish the route
- **Is the decision appealable now?** Final judgment, or interlocutory order (permission often required)? Appeal as of right, or permission to appeal?
- **Which court hears it, by when?** The deadline usually runs from the date of the order or entry, not from when you learned of it. Compute the date in writing.
- **Is there a further tier?** (second appeal, supreme court review, cassation-type review). What does it review (law only, or also facts)?
- **Is a stay needed?** Does the appeal suspend enforcement, or must you apply for a stay?

## 2. The logic of a ground of appeal (any system)
A ground is not "we disagree". It is: **the specific error → the legal category of the error → the standard of review → where it appears in the record (volume, page) → why it mattered to the result → what we ask the court to do**.
- **Errors of law:** reviewed with little or no deference (de novo).
- **Errors of fact:** reviewed with deference (clear error, or "plainly wrong"); do not lead with them unless the finding has no evidence behind it.
- **Discretionary decisions** (case management, costs, interim relief): reviewed for abuse of discretion or a failure to exercise it properly; hard to win.
- **Procedural unfairness:** the right to be heard, notice, a biased tribunal: structural and often decisive.
- **Jurisdictional defects:** cannot be waived.

## 3. Preservation of error
The first filter on every ground: was it raised below, in time and in the proper form? An argument that was not raised is usually waived or reviewed only for plain error. List the points at risk and, if the record is still open, cure them now (apply to amend, to reopen, to supplement the record).

## 4. Fresh evidence and new arguments
- New evidence on appeal is allowed only on strict conditions (it could not have been obtained with reasonable diligence; it would probably have affected the result; it is credible) `[VERIFY]`.
- New points of law that rest on the existing record are treated more leniently than new factual issues.
- If the appeal depends on a fact that was never proved below, the better route may be a review or a fresh claim, not an appeal.

## 5. Late appeals and extensions of time
- Apply immediately; explain the delay precisely and with evidence; show prejudice or its absence; show the merits.
- The courts apply their own tests (strict ones for delay that is the party's own); do not assume leniency.

## 6. Evidence for appeals
- Build the appeal bundle from the record: transcripts, judgments, pleadings, exhibits; order the transcript early.
- Cite the record precisely. An appeal that cannot point to the page loses.

## 7. Costs and conduct on appeal
- Adverse costs on a failed appeal; security for costs; the effect of a successful appeal on costs below.
- Conduct that counts against you: raising new points late, bad-faith or hopeless grounds.

## 8. When NOT to appeal
- The error was not preserved; the standard of review is hopeless; the win would be pyrrhic.
- A favorable part of the judgment may be put at risk by the other side's cross-appeal.
- The judgment can be complied with at a low cost, or settled.
- An unsuccessful appeal creates a precedent or estoppel that hurts the client elsewhere.
- Time: enforcement of a favorable judgment is delayed.
Say this plainly, with numbers, when it is the rational advice.

## 9. Further review and finality
- Know what the next tier will accept (often only questions of law or of general importance).
- Preserve for the next tier at every step, and do not let the appeal record narrow the issues you may need later.
- Set aside or review proceedings for fraud or new evidence are a separate route with strict conditions.
