# Examples of Devil's Advocate reviews

Samples to follow for tone, structure, and depth. All examples are fictional cases and show only the form of the work. Where a rule or case is named, it is a placeholder to be verified in the real jurisdiction.

---

## Example 1. Legal matter

**Request:** "We are suing a contractor for damages for missing the construction deadline. The contractor delivered our warehouse four months late, so we could not start trading. Lost profit: $1.4M. We have the contract, the acceptance certificate, and revenue statements of comparable sites. Criticize our position."

### Verdict

The $1.4M claim rests almost entirely on "revenue statements of comparable sites". That is the thread the opponent will cut with the first objection. Everything else (the delay, its cause) is easier, but without proof of the amount the claim shrinks to the contractual late-delivery penalty, a fraction of what you pleaded.

### Main vulnerabilities (descending order of danger)

**1. Lost profit cannot be proven with "statements of comparables"**
Courts in most systems demand proof of lost profit with reasonable certainty, plus the concrete steps the claimant took to earn it. Someone else's revenue is a hypothesis, not evidence. The opponent will cite the governing standard `[VERIFY: your jurisdiction's certainty standard]` and the court will leave the claim without an amount. Move to the contractual penalty, or commission an expert report on lost profit built on your own traffic, prices, and margins.

**2. Causation "delay → loss" is attacked through counter-delays**
Any contractor will answer: we were late because the owner did not provide the site, did not hand over materials, did not approve changes on time. If your correspondence contains even one late response of yours, the delay becomes mutual and the chain "your breach → my loss" breaks. Pull all correspondence and the payment and progress certificates before the opponent does it for you.

**3. Readiness to trade on the delivery date is your burden**
To claim lost profit you must show that on the day the warehouse should have been delivered you were ready to trade: stock bought, staff hired, customer contracts signed. If not, the loss would not have arisen even with timely delivery. The opponent will show it through the absence of your counterparty contracts dated before the delay.

**4. Penalty and damages are mixed**
If the contract has a late-delivery penalty, in many systems it is exclusive or offsetting `[VERIFY]`. Then damages are recoverable only above the penalty. The opponent will point this out, and the court will deduct the penalty from your $1.4M whether or not you pleaded it.

**5. Has the limitation period run on part of the claim**
The delay lasted four months, so the period for the earliest losses began to run at the same time. Check the filing date: the period may already have expired.

### Attack on what you think is your strength

You probably lean on the acceptance certificate and the contract: "the delay is obvious". That is the most vulnerable spot, because the opponent will not contest that fact. They will agree: yes, we were late. Then they will break the amount and the cause. Contract and certificate without a loss calculation give you only the contractual penalty, a fraction of $1.4M.

### Questions you cannot answer

1. Which contracts with buyers or tenants did you have signed on the planned delivery date?
2. What stock was bought and where was it stored during the delay? Did you pay for storage and include it in the claim (and does it duplicate the lost profit)?
3. Which "comparable sites" are the statements from, and how are they comparable (location, area, traffic)?
4. Did you run your own revenue projection before the project, in a business plan or in correspondence with your bank? Those numbers may differ from $1.4M and will be used against you.
5. Did you send the contractor interim notices of claim? If not, why were you silent for four months if the loss was obvious?
6. Why did you not terminate when the delay began and bring in another contractor? Is that a failure to mitigate? `[VERIFY: mitigation rule]`

### Worst case

The court finds the delay, rejects lost profit for failure to prove the amount and causation, and awards the contractual penalty of $100–150K. You spend 18 months and $150K in costs on what a demand letter could have resolved in a month.

### What saves you

Commission an economic expert report specifically on your site, with your price lists, your contracts, your sales channel. Pull the correspondence and close the counter-breach question. Rebuild the claim: penalty + real expenses + lost profit, clearly separated. Otherwise, fold back to the penalty.

*Authorities above are from memory; verify before relying on them.*

## Example 2. Business decision

**Request:** "I want to launch a lunch-delivery subscription for lawyers in downtown office towers. $18 a day, five days a week. The market is huge, almost no competition, lawyers earn well and have no time to choose food. Tear it apart."

### Verdict

You confused "I see no direct competitors" with "there is no market". Lawyers in premium towers have already solved lunch: delivery from restaurants through aggregators, cafés in the building, an assistant who orders. Your service has to displace those solutions, not fill an empty space.

### Main vulnerabilities

**1. False premise of no competition**
A competitor is not whoever sells "a lunch subscription to lawyers". A competitor is any way a lawyer gets lunch today: delivery apps, the office kitchen, the café in the tower, the assistant's order. All of them already offer flexibility (a different cuisine every day) that you will not have. You must offer what they do not, and prove it matters.

**2. Unit economics at $18 a day**
$18 is a restaurant price. Out of it: $5–6 premium food cost, $2.50–3.50 packaging and delivery, $2–2.50 labor for packing and logistics, $1+ payment fees and IT. That leaves $5–6 of margin. To break even you need customer acquisition cost under $45–50 and retention of 3+ months. A product with no habit (lawyers like variety, you offer a fixed menu) will have low retention.

**3. The lawyer's habit is not your habit**
A lawyer may fly to a hearing, go to a meeting, stay in a conference room, skip lunch, eat out with a client. A subscription assumes that every workday from noon to two they are in the office and ready to eat what you bring. That is not a lawyer's life.

**4. "Lawyers earn well" is not "lawyers spend on lunch"**
High earners are often very frugal on regular small spending. $18 × 22 working days = $396 a month on lunches. That is visible even at a high income. Many will prefer $8–10 at the café downstairs and save "premium food" for weekend restaurants.

**5. Logistics for 5–10 towers**
Economics need order density, which means concentration in 3–5 specific towers. That puts you in direct conflict with the in-house cafés of those towers, which have administrative leverage and ground-floor leases. How do you pass security and deliver at lunch hour when every courier is stuck in elevator queues?

**6. No protection against copying**
The idea is trivially copied by any large delivery platform or restaurant group. As soon as you show the model works, you are copied within a quarter with better logistics and a known brand. The question is not "how to launch" but "what is my unburnable advantage in a year".

### Attack on your strength

You consider market size your strength: "there are thousands of lawyers". That is the weak side: the market exists, so everyone who can move food has already entered it. The absence of a specialized "lunch for lawyers" service is not a free niche but a signal that vertical segmentation by profession does not work, because a lawyer eats the same as the marketing manager next door.

### Questions you cannot answer

1. How many lunches a day must subscribe for break-even? What share of lawyers in the target towers is that?
2. Why a subscription and not pay-per-order? What makes the subscription better for the customer than a delivery app where they choose on the spot?
3. What do you do if a customer is at hearings three days in a row and does not come to the office: refund? If yes, the economics collapse. If no, churn.
4. Which chef and which cuisine do you work with? What separates your "premium" from ordinary delivery in a lawyer's eyes?
5. How do you handle allergies, diets, keto, gluten-free? In the legal profession these are widespread.
6. What is your customer acquisition cost from preliminary tests, and what is the channel?
7. What happens if a major tower bans outside couriers from going up to the floors?
8. Where do the first 100 customers come from? Specific names, or only "lawyers in the city"?

### Worst case

You launch on your own $500K–800K. In the first two months, 40 customers (friends and friends of friends); in month three, retention drops to 15. Acquisition cost lands at $120–150, lifetime value at $90–100. After six months the money runs out, you try to raise a round, but investors see the same numbers and decline. Two months later you close, having paid the chefs and couriers termination costs.

### What saves you

Rethink radically. Not "food for lawyers", but a specific pain scenario that today's solutions fail: for example, a hot lunch delivered to the conference room 20 minutes after ordering, guaranteed (which delivery apps cannot do because of restaurant prep). Or a corporate package "the firm pays partners' lunches" integrated with accounting. But that is a different product, and it must be tested before launch, not after.

## What the examples show

- **The verdict hits the main point in 2–3 sentences** and says at once what the position will collapse on.
- **Vulnerabilities are concrete**: not "there are proof problems", but "statements of comparables will not pass as evidence: here is the standard, here is how it will be used".
- **The mechanics of the attack are explained**: not "the opponent will object", but "the opponent will cite the standard and the court will leave the claim without an amount".
- **Questions are not rhetorical**: they are ones the user cannot answer right now.
- **The worst case is drawn in detail**, so you can see exactly how it happens.
- **What saves you is brief and honest**: if the chances are small, say so.
- **Tone: no hysteria, no jabs, no "if I were you"**. Just a hard, concrete review.
