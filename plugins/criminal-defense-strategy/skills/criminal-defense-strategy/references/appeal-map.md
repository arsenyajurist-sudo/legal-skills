# Appeal map

Appeal systems differ widely: tiers, permission requirements, deadlines, and standards. Establish the forum's route first, then use the logic below. Mark every specific rule `[VERIFY]`.

## 1. Establish the route
- **Appeals against conviction, sentence, or both?** Rights of appeal and the need for permission.
- **Which court, by when?** The deadline usually runs from the date of the verdict, sentence, or order: compute the date in writing and calendar it.
- **Is there a further tier?** (second appeal, supreme court review, cassation-type review, references by a review commission). What does it review: law only, or facts too?
- **Is interim relief needed?** Bail pending appeal, stay of a confiscation or financial order.

## 2. The logic of a ground of appeal
A ground is not "we disagree". It is: **the specific error → the legal category of the error → the standard of review → where it appears in the record → why it mattered to the result → what we ask the court to do.**
- **Errors of law:** reviewed with little or no deference.
- **Findings of fact and credibility:** reviewed with deference; do not lead with them unless there was no evidence or the finding was irrational.
- **Evidential rulings and discretion:** abuse of discretion or error of principle; the prejudice has to be shown.
- **Misdirection or inadequate jury instructions.**
- **Procedural unfairness:** the right to a fair hearing, disclosure failures, bias, inadequate representation, juror issues.
- **Unsafe or unreasonable verdict** where the system allows that ground.
- **Sentence:** manifestly excessive, wrong in principle, wrong range, errors of fact on which the sentence rests.

## 3. Preservation of error
The first filter on every ground: was it raised in time and in proper form at the trial? An argument not raised is usually waived or reviewed only for plain error. List points at risk and, where the record is still open, cure them now.

## 4. Structural errors and reversal
Some errors lead to reversal without proof of prejudice (denial of counsel, a biased judge, a fundamental defect in the charge or jurisdiction) `[VERIFY]`. Others require showing that the error affected the outcome (harmless-error or proviso analysis). Know which tests apply.

## 5. Fresh evidence
New evidence on appeal is admitted only on strict conditions (it could not have been obtained at trial with reasonable diligence; it is credible; it may have affected the verdict) `[VERIFY]`. If the real problem is a fact never proved at trial, consider a post-conviction or review route.

## 6. Ineffective assistance and post-conviction relief
Where available, claims of defective representation, newly discovered evidence, constitutional violations, or wrongful conviction reviews: these have their own deadlines and thresholds. Calendar them early.

## 7. Risk of a worse outcome
- An appeal by the defense may open the sentence to increase in some systems, or provoke a cross-appeal by the prosecution.
- A retrial after reversal can end worse than the original sentence.
- Explain these risks to the client and obtain their decision.

## 8. When not to appeal
- The error was not preserved; the standard of review is hopeless; the benefit is pyrrhic.
- The risk of a heavier outcome outweighs the likely gain.
- A negotiated outcome or early release is more valuable than a long appeal.
- Time: the client's detention continues while the appeal is pending; weigh the credit for time served.
Say so plainly, with numbers.

## 9. Working with the record
- Order the transcript and the exhibits early; check them for gaps and errors, and correct the record where possible.
- Build the appellate bundle from the record; cite precisely (page and line).
- Objections to the record or minutes: raise promptly.
- An appeal that cannot point to the page loses.
